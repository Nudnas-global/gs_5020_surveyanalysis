01   Overview

What you are doing — and why

In the lab exercises you practised quantitative techniques on a clean, well-known dataset. This assignment asks you to apply the same methods to messy, real-world data — a survey of graduate students about their career ambitions, perceived opportunities, and anticipated challenges.

You will choose a focused research question, select appropriate statistical tests, carry out the analysis in Excel, and write up your findings as a concise research report. The goal is not to produce a sweeping study; it is to demonstrate that you can reason carefully about which test to use, execute it correctly, and interpret the result honestly.

The dataset in brief. The file Graduate_Students_Future_Ambitions_Survey.xlsx contains responses from 30 graduate students at a single university. The survey was completed in May 2026 and covers four thematic sections: (A) demographic profile, (B) career aspirations, (C) perceived opportunities, and (D) anticipated challenges. All responses are anonymous.

03   Research questions

Choose one research question

Select exactly one of the six options below. Your chosen question determines which statistical tests you will run. Each question is designed to require at least two distinct tests from the techniques covered in the lab — you must run both and report both.

You may adapt the wording of your chosen question slightly to sharpen the focus, but the core variables and required tests must remain the same. Note your chosen question and RQ number at the top of your report.

RQ 1

Does gender shape career-direction clarity and planning level among graduate students?

Variables: b3_clarity (scale), b4_plan (ordinal), a2_gender (binary)

Required tests:

Independent t-test Mann-Whitney U

Compare b3_clarity across genders with a t-test; compare the ordinal b4_plan with Mann-Whitney U.

RQ 2

Do students at different qualification levels differ in how confidently they view their career prospects?

Variables: c1_qual_doors (scale), c5_job_market (ordinal), a4_study_level (3 groups)

Required tests:

One-way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis

Use ANOVA for the scale variable and Kruskal-Wallis for the ordinal variable across degree / masters / doctoral groups.

RQ 3

Is career-direction clarity related to qualification confidence and network awareness?

Variables: b3_clarity, c1_qual_doors, c2_network_aware (all scale 1–5)

Required tests:

Pearson correlation Spearman ρ

Calculate Pearson r for both pairs; then re-run as Spearman ρ to check robustness. Compare and explain any differences.

RQ 4

Does employment status during study affect students' career-clarity scores and job-market perceptions?

Variables: b3_clarity (scale), c5_job_market (ordinal), a8_employment (categories)

Required tests:

One-way ANOVA Kruskal-Wallis

Group students by employment status (fulltime / parttime / none / not_related) and compare across both outcome variables.

RQ 5

Do full-time and part-time students differ in their perceived qualification value and network awareness?

Variables: c1_qual_doors, c2_network_aware (scale), a7_study_mode (recode to 2 groups)

Required tests:

Independent t-test Pearson correlation

Compare the two groups with a t-test for each scale variable; then explore whether qualification confidence and network awareness correlate regardless of mode.

RQ 6

Does field of study (STEM vs. non-STEM) predict how students rate career clarity and opportunity access?

Variables: b3_clarity (scale), c2_network_aware (scale), a5_field_study (recode to STEM / non-STEM)

Required tests:

Independent t-test Mann-Whitney U

Compare b3_clarity with an independent t-test; use Mann-Whitney U for c2_network_aware given possible skew. State your assumptions.

04   Analysis requirements

What your analysis must include

Regardless of which research question you choose, your analysis must satisfy all of the following requirements.

Descriptive statistics (for each variable used)

  • Sample size (n), mean, standard deviation for any scale/continuous variable
  • Frequency counts and percentages for any categorical or ordinal variable
  • At least one clearly labelled table or chart to summarise your sample

Assumption checking (before each inferential test)

  • State which assumptions apply to your chosen test (e.g. normality, equal variances)
  • Use at least one method to check normality for scale variables (histogram, or note sample size and invoke CLT)
  • Justify your choice of parametric vs. non-parametric test for each analysis

Inferential tests

  • Run both required tests as specified in your chosen RQ
  • Report the test statistic, degrees of freedom (where applicable), and exact p-value for each test
  • State the null and alternative hypothesis for each test
  • For correlation: report both r and r², and test for statistical significance
  • Use α = 0.05 as your significance threshold throughout

Interpretation

  • For each test: state whether you reject or fail to reject the null hypothesis, and say what that means in plain English about graduate students
  • Discuss any limitations (small n, non-normal data, single-university sample)
  • Do not over-claim causation from a cross-sectional survey
05   Report structure

How to structure your report

Your report should follow the structure below. Sections 2–4 carry most of the marks.

1

Introduction ~200 words

State your chosen research question (RQ number and full text). Briefly explain why it is worth investigating based on what the survey measures. End with a clear statement of your two null hypotheses, one per test.

2

Data & Method ~300 words

Describe the dataset (who completed it, when, how many respondents). Identify your outcome and grouping variables, including how you recoded any variables (e.g. STEM / non-STEM). Justify which tests you chose and confirm which assumptions you checked. Show a descriptive statistics table here.

3

Results ~600 words

Present each test in turn. Lead with the test name, null hypothesis, key statistic, and p-value. Include a formatted results table or extracted Excel output in an appendix (referenced here). Write a one- to two-sentence plain-English interpretation for each result. For correlations, include a scatterplot.

4

Discussion ~400 words

Synthesise what your two results together say about graduate students' ambitions and experiences. Identify at least two limitations of the analysis. Suggest one follow-up question that your findings raise.

5

Conclusion ~100 words

Summarise your findings in no more than a short paragraph. No new information.

A

Appendix — Excel output not counted in word limit

Paste your raw Excel output (Data Analysis dialog results, CORREL formula cells, etc.) as screenshots or a formatted table. Label each exhibit clearly: e.g. Appendix A1: ANOVA output for RQ 2, b3_clarity by study level.

07   Guidance

Practical tips

Working with the small sample

With only 30 respondents, some group comparisons (e.g. doctoral students vs. undergraduates) will have very small subgroups. This is fine — it is a real-world limitation you should name in your Discussion. Do not exclude groups to get cleaner results. Use what you have, report it honestly, and explain what a larger sample would allow you to say.

Recoding variables

Several questions require you to create a helper column in Excel. For example, for RQ 6 you need a binary STEM / non-STEM column. Add a new column labelled clearly (e.g. stem_binary), use an IF formula to recode, and describe your recoding rule in the Method section. Always work on a copy of the data sheet — do not overwrite the originals.

Reporting numbers

Round statistics to two decimal places in your report text (e.g. t(28) = 2.14, p = 0.04). For very small p-values write p < 0.001 rather than listing many decimal places. Always pair a test statistic with its p-value — never report one without the other.

A word on sample size and power

A non-significant result (p ≥ 0.05) with n = 30 does not prove there is no difference — it may simply mean the study was underpowered to detect a small effect. If your tests come back non-significant, discuss this as a limitation, not as proof of the null hypothesis being true.

Common mistakes to avoid: (1) Running the wrong test for the variable type — check the lab guide's decision flowchart. (2) Forgetting to include Excel output in the appendix. (3) Confusing statistical significance with practical importance. (4) Claiming causation from a cross-sectional survey. (5) Reporting p = 0.000 — always write p < 0.001 instead.

Academic integrity

You must conduct your own analysis independently. You may discuss the dataset and research questions with classmates, but your written report, Excel calculations, and interpretations must be entirely your own work.

AI tools: You may use AI assistants to help you understand statistical concepts, but you may not use them to generate your results, write your interpretations, or draft sections of your report. Your submitted work must reflect your own analytical reasoning. Suspected contract cheating or AI-generated submission will be referred under the university's academic misconduct policy.

Attach a signed declaration of originality as the first page of your PDF submission.
